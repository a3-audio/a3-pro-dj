A³ · MJ Lab · 2026-10-08 · a night of simulation

MJ Lab

How should sound fly through the A³ dome so that a dance floor follows it, and does not get bored or dizzy? A virtual floor, a crowd that listens with a model built from the psychoacoustics literature, and the FPV gravity flight from the phase-2 plan, run for 4852 seeded sets.

This is a model, not evidence. Every listener in it is a handful of equations taken from lab studies of single listeners in quiet rooms, plus rules I made up and say so. It can show what follows from those assumptions and which design choices are fragile. It cannot show what a real floor does. Section 12 lists what only a listening test on the rig can decide.

The short version

  1. With the plan's start values the planets eat the ships. A group pulls harder than a ship can steer back (gravity cap 1.5 against a steering cap of 0.3), so every ship falls into the nearest group and spins a few centimetres overhead: motion is heard 10% of the time instead of 92%. Turning G down does not help — there is a cliff, not a slope. The fix is a ratio: gravityMax ≤ steerMax. Under it the same groups bend the path and let it go. This was the most robust result of the night: the plan's cap lost in every seed of every listener variant. (Gating gravity to beat 1, finding 5, fixes it independently: three free beats a bar let every ship climb out.)
  2. Bending in space halves boredom, but the planets do not buy attention — and once the ships breathe, they cost. Under the cap, groups cut "heard but tuned out" from 0.68 to 0.35 (all listener variants), yet engagement stays level and the least-served quarter usually loses: ships go where the groups already are, and the wells act as brakes — a ship climbing out of one with a capped steer slows down and is pulled in towards the zenith. With the breath (finding 3) doing the anti-boredom work, a set without bodies scored 0.332 against 0.270 with the closed loop (no bodies ahead in 18 of 18 listener variants). Keep the groups as the DJ's way of saying where, light and spread out — not as the cure for the circle.
  3. Contrast in time works: let the ships breathe. The cheapest attention in the model is a ship that stands still for one beat — beat 4 of every bar — and restarts on the one: +0.056 ± 0.008, 6 of 6 seeds engagement for little fatigue (+0.025 ± 0.005, 6 of 6 seeds), from 100 to 174 BPM. It gained in 18 of 18 listener variants. Half-beat stops are too short to be integrated by the ear; two stops a bar become a stutter; long holds plus doublings gain less and tire the floor several times faster. The stops sit on the grid, so the flight stays in time.
  4. Two moving ships, a cruise of about 50-75 °/s, a low path, a wide band with energy above 4 kHz. One ship is missed too often; three or four tire the floor. One lap per 4 bars; with the ships breathing that holds from 100 to 174 BPM (a 2-bar lap then turns every restart into a sprint). A path that comes down towards the horizon is heard better than one near the zenith. 6-8 kHz at 2 octaves or 3 kHz at 4 octaves; 1 octave or less, or a centre at 1 kHz or below, loses most of the effect.
  5. The beat pulse is inaudible as a beat. Breathing gravity +30 % / +60 % moves a ship by under one degree per beat (3.1° → 3.7° of beat wobble); research puts a heard deviation at 20-30°. Three times the depth changes nothing. The music link the model can hear is a move that starts on the one: the breath, or gravity switched on only during beat 1, which also makes the planets six times cheaper (section 8f).
The recommended set, in one line: the plan's flight with gravityMax = steerMax, two moving ships, each standing still on beat 4 of every bar, guest groups at half mass whose gravity acts only during beat 1, and a moving band of 8 kHz at 2 octaves (or 3 kHz at 4). Final check, 12 seeds × 96 bars: engagement 0.329 against 0.008 for the plan as written and 0.195 for a bare circle in the same band; least-served quarter 0.246; fatigue 0.112. It beat the bare circle and the plan as written in all 18 listener variants and every one of their 72 seeds (section 8f).

4852 seeded runs of 64-192 bars each in 51 rounds, 02:35-06:35 CEST. Recommended defaults: section 11.

1. The question

The maintainer, on the gravity flight: ships that only go round their path sound boring, so the guest groups on the floor should act as planets that bend, fling and capture them. The question for this night was the MJ's: which movements actually pull listeners in, which ones go unnoticed, and which ones wear a floor out? And, under it, the fear: does a gravity field really stop the circling, or does it just move the circle?

What I could do without touching the rig: build the floor in software, port the flight model from the plan exactly, give each simulated guest the hearing limits the literature reports, let them walk towards what they like and away from what tires them, let their groups become the planets, and run it many times with different seeds.

2. The model

The room and the flight

A round floor under a dome; 8 speakers on a ring at ear height and 4 above at 45°; room edge at 6 m (radius 1 in floor units). Guests stand inside 85 % of the radius. Up to four ships fly the phase-2 gravity model: FlightTuning, gravityAt, gravityPulse and rabbitAt are ported line for line from the C++ in feat/fpv-gravity; the ship step, wander, separation and escort follow the plan's text (they were not written yet). Fixed step of 1/64 beat (1/128 checked: same results), time in beats, 126 BPM, all randomness seeded. A ship's floor point becomes a direction exactly as HeightMap::mapTo3D does with default elevation (room edge at 26°, centre at the zenith).

Per sample (every 1/16 note) each guest gets engagement = attended interest × (1 − fatigue) − blur − 0.3·fatigue. That is the headline metric. Next to it: bottom quarter (mean engagement of the 25 % least-served guests), perceived (share of time any motion is above threshold), boredom (share of heard time with no recent event), fatigue.

How the crowd moves, and the closed loop

72 guests start in three clumps plus scattered people. Each walks slowly (at most ~5 cm/s) towards ships whose motion it finds salient and pleasant and away from blur and fatigue, explores at random while dissatisfied, looks for company when alone and keeps 36 cm personal space. Every bar the automatic MJ clusters the floor (people within 0.7 m of each other) and turns groups of 4+ into gravity bodies: 4-7 people mass 1, 8-14 mass 2, 15+ mass 3. The ships bend towards them, the guests walk towards the ships: that is the closed loop the plan asks for. guess for every number in this paragraph.

Limits I know of

  • No real music: one band per ship with a fixed level. The model prefers high, wide bands because they localise best; whether a track has enough energy up there is a track question.
  • No room acoustics beyond the precedence pull; no subwoofers; no visual cue from the DJ.
  • The crowd's walking and fatigue rules are invented. Section 9 changes them and checks which conclusions survive.
  • Engagement is a single number built from my own weights. Rankings matter here, not values.
  • Over long sets the model's crowd walks itself into one big group whatever the ships do; a real floor has walls, a bar and friends. Crowd-shape results are the weakest part of the model.

4. Gravity: from capture to bend

With the plan's start values the pull of a group of mass 1 is stronger than the ship's steering inside about 0.25 of the room's radius, and a crowd of mass 3 reaches 0.4 — which, with the big path at 0.56-0.84, is everywhere. Every ship ends up on a group and circles in the softening core. Seen from the floor that is a sound parked overhead.

6. The beat pulse

The plan's pulse multiplies gravity by up to 1.3 on each beat and 1.6 on the one. The audible quantity is how far a ship moves off its smooth path within a beat. Measured as the RMS of a ship's azimuth minus its one-beat moving average: 3.1° with the pulse off, 3.7° with the plan's pulse, 3.7° at three times the depth. Research puts the threshold for a heard deviation at 20-30° (Grantham; the research note's B2/B5). The pulse makes the bends happen a little earlier in the beat, but nobody will hear it as a beat.

Moves in time beat moves in space. A hold makes the next start an event for everyone at once, and a doubling is the smallest speed change the ear reliably hears (Carlile & Best; Locke 2016). The later cadence rounds (below) found that the stop does not need to be long: one beat is enough. Kicks add events too, but they move the band over the crowd and add more fatigue than interest. Halving makes motion drop below threshold at the sides.

8. The closed loop and the crowd

Guests who follow the sound concentrate: with silence 67% of the floor stands in groups of 4+, with ships they ignore 76%, with ships they follow 84%, and when they walk three times as fast 95% (largest group 93%). Bodies add little to this; the walking does it. Fairness is where bodies cost: the least-served quarter drops from 0.129 to 0.099, because the ships spend their time over the groups. Halving all masses gives some of it back (0.114) at the price of boredom.

10. What the MJ concludes

  1. Gravity is a bending tool with a hard ceiling. Never let the pull exceed what the ship can steer back with. Above that line the sound parks; below it the path bends and recovers. Make it a rule in FlightTuning, not a tuning value: gravityMax = steerMax. Stiffer steering fixes the plan's tracking error (0.19 behind the rabbit) but is not a gain in itself.
  2. The circle is not the enemy; an unbroken circle is. What un-bores a floor in this model is a change it can hear: a short stop and a restart on the one — the breath, one beat every second bar — or a doubling. Bending towards groups changes the geometry, but from the floor it mostly turns azimuth motion into overhead motion.
  3. Put the moves on bar lines, not the beat into the physics. The pulse is harmless, but it is not the music link. A move that starts on the one is.
  4. Two ships move; the rest wait. With moves on, a third moving ship already costs more than it gives.
  5. Cruise at one lap per 4 bars. Without stops the best cruise is an angle per second (50-75 °/s, so 2 bars below ~110 BPM); with the breath, 4 bars at every tempo tried. Fly the big path low.
  6. Move the top of the mix. 6-8 kHz at 2 octaves, or 3 kHz at 4. Never narrower than an octave, never centred at 1 kHz or below, if the path should be followed.
  7. Let the planets say where, not fight the boredom. Once the ships breathe, groups as gravity cost attention in this model. Keep them light (half the plan's masses) and spread, and let the DJ use them to point the sound at a part of the room.
  8. Escort wide or not at all, and only as a gesture. At the plan's radius it parks the ship over the group.
  9. Watch who is left out, and watch the floor tire. Groups pull ships and ships serve groups; lighter masses keep the edges in. The moves are worth it only while the floor has the stamina, so an MJ that eases off when the floor tires is the safe default (section 8d).

12. What only a listening test can decide

  • Whether "boredom" exists the way the model says. The 20 % interest a steady circle keeps is my number (section 9 shows the rankings survive 0.05 and 0.5, but the size of every gain depends on it). Test: 16 bars of bare orbit, ask "still interesting?" at bars 4, 8, 12 and 16.
  • Fatigue and dizziness. No published threshold exists. The ship-count result rests on it. Block 6 of the research note's listening test (1 vs 2 vs 4 ships) decides it.
  • Whether diving over the crowd is heard. The model says a 3 kHz band overhead is nearly static to the people under it. Test: one ship patrolling vs pulled over a group, 3 kHz vs 8 kHz.
  • Energy in the band. The sim prefers 6-8 kHz because it localises best; a dark track may have nothing there. C0 (measure Isolator3 in Hz) comes first.
  • The breath. Whether a ship that stops for one beat every bar reads as groove or as a dropout. The model's whole case for it is that a stop and a restart on the grid are events; a real ear may hear a glitch. Test: 16 bars of plain orbit against 16 bars with the breath, same band, same level, listeners not told which.
  • Off-centre listening. The precedence pull is modelled crudely; the rim of the floor may hear far less than the model says.
  • How a crowd actually moves. Nothing in the walking rules is measured. Watch the floor.
